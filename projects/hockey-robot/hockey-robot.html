Autonomous Hockey Robot

Built an autonomous robot that locates a puck, captures it, finds the goal, and scores. Designed and fabricated the chassis, wired the electronics, and developed the control logic for JHU's JHockey competition.

Overview

For the Mechatronics final project at Johns Hopkins, our team of four built an autonomous hockey robot. The robot had to locate an orange puck on the arena, capture it, identify the correct goal, and score without any human input.

I was responsible for the CAD design, laser cutting, mechanical assembly, wiring, and contributed to the control logic. The robot competed in the JHockey tournament and passed all required demonstrations.

Mechanical Design

I designed the robot chassis in CAD and laser cut it from 3/16 inch acrylic sheet. The design built on a template from earlier labs with key modifications:

  • Wheel shields to protect the motors from puck and robot collisions
  • Bracket-shaped front attachment to capture and hold the puck
  • Slot in the bracket arm to mount the PING sensor for puck detection
  • Curved edges on the bracket to help the robot slide off walls

The bracket gap was initially 12 cm but was reduced to 9 cm for better puck control and to meet size requirements.

Electronics

I wired all the sensors and actuators to the Arduino Mega. The system included:

  • Pixy2 camera for color detection (orange puck, green goal)
  • PING ultrasonic sensor to confirm puck possession
  • ZigBee module for arena position tracking via ArUco tags
  • BNO055 IMU for orientation and stuck detection
  • Two DC motors with a MAX14870 motor shield
  • 8.4V battery pack for power

The breadboard was replaced with a protoboard for reliability during competition.

Software & Strategy

The robot used a state machine to break down the task into manageable steps. I contributed to the control logic and PID tuning.

State Machine

  • Sweep: Spin in place until the Pixy camera detects the orange puck centered in frame
  • Chase: Drive toward the puck using PID to keep it centered. PING sensor detects when puck is captured
  • Green Sweep: Rotate to find the green goal. Use position data to determine which direction to turn
  • Drive: Dribble the puck toward the goal while keeping the green target centered
  • End: Stop when the robot reaches the goal coordinates

Challenges & Solutions

  • Puck slipped during turns: reduced turning speed when holding the puck
  • Robot got stuck on walls: added IMU check to detect no yaw change, then jolt forward to escape
  • Both goals are green: used position tracking to determine which goal to target based on travel direction

Competition

The robot passed all three required demonstrations: locating the puck, capturing it, and scoring in the correct goal.

In the JHockey tournament, the first match ended in a stalemate and went to shootouts. Our straightforward strategy worked well in shootouts. The second match was against a heavier robot with higher torque motors that overpowered ours.

  • How to design parts for laser cutting with proper tolerances and assembly features
  • Wiring sensors and actuators to a microcontroller and debugging electrical issues
  • Implementing a state machine for autonomous robot behavior
  • PID tuning for motor control and camera-based tracking
