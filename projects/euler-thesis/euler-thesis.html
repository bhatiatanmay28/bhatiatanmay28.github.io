Diesel to Electric Truck Conversion

Worked on converting a 7.5 ton Tata T7 Ultra diesel truck to run on electricity at Euler Motors. Performed motor and gearbox sizing calculations and learned the theory behind EV systems, wire harness design, sheet metal design, and plastic design.

Timeline Jan 2023 - May 2024
Role Mechanical Engineer
Company Euler Motors
Location New Delhi, India

Overview

Euler Motors is a commercial electric vehicle manufacturer in New Delhi. The goal of this project was to retrofit a 7.5 ton diesel truck with an electric powertrain. The target was a top speed of 80 kmph and a range of at least 100 km.

I performed motor and gearbox sizing calculations for this project. I also learned the theory and design principles for high voltage electrical architecture, wire harness routing, sheet metal design, and plastic design.

Motor Sizing & Gearbox Design

The first step was to benchmark the diesel truck. The team recorded performance data at different payloads: zero, 50%, full, and overloaded. I used Python to filter noise and extract usable values from the logged data.

Forces to Overcome

To size the motor, I calculated the forces the powertrain must overcome:

  • Air drag: increases with velocity squared, depends on frontal area and drag coefficient
  • Rolling resistance: affected by tire pressure, road surface, and vehicle weight
  • Gradient resistance: force needed to climb slopes
  • Acceleration force: mass times acceleration

Drive Cycle Analysis

I used the WLTP drive cycle to simulate real world conditions. The cycle defines velocity, acceleration, and gradient at each point in time. This helped find the peak power and torque required without over-designing the system.

Motor Selection

A PMSM (Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motor) was selected for its high torque density, wide efficiency band, low noise, and regenerative braking capability.

Gearbox Design

I calculated the gear ratio based on top speed (80 kmph) and motor max RPM (7000 RPM). The gear ratio came out to about 14:1. I then designed the gears using:

  • Beam strength equations (Lewis form factor)
  • Wear strength equations (load stress factor)
  • Buckingham's equation for dynamic loads

A two-stage reduction gearbox with a differential was planned for compact packaging.

High Voltage Electrical Architecture

I learned the components that make up the high voltage system in an electric vehicle:

  • Motor controller: Uses Field Oriented Control to manage torque and speed based on throttle input, brake input, and sensor data
  • On-board charger (OBC): Converts AC power to DC for slow charging
  • DC-DC converter: Steps down high voltage to 12V for lights, horn, and accessories
  • Contactors: Electrically controlled switches that open/close the high voltage circuit for safety
  • Battery cooling system: Maintains optimal cell temperature using coolant loops
  • E-compressor: Provides compressed air for pneumatic brakes (replaces engine-driven compressor)
  • E-HPS: Electric hydraulic power steering (replaces engine-driven pump)

For the retrofit, the team verified that the existing low voltage electronics would work without the engine by disconnecting sensors one by one and checking the ECU sanity check.

Wire Harness Design

I learned the principles of wire harness design for electric vehicles.

High Voltage Cables

  • Shielding (braided or foil) to reduce electromagnetic interference
  • Insulation classes (A through F) based on temperature rating
  • Minimum bend radius: 3x outer diameter for static routing, 6x for dynamic
  • Drain wire to ground the shielding

Low Voltage Cables

  • Bundled together and routed through chassis
  • Thick, thin, or ultra-thin wall configurations
  • CAN protocol for sensor communication

Routing Principles

  • Shortest path to reduce voltage drop and weight
  • Clips every 150-250 mm to hold harness in place
  • Corrugated tubes for protection from dust, moisture, and damage
  • Cable glands and grommets at panel entry points
  • Keep wires away from hot components

CAD Workflow

I routed the harness in CATIA V5 using the Electrical Harness Assembly workbench. I defined connectors, ring terminals, and support clips. The software generates a spline path following the minimum bend radius. I then flattened the 3D harness to create a 2D manufacturing drawing.

Sheet Metal Design

I learned the design rules for sheet metal components used in housings and mounts.

Key Parameters

  • Thickness: Controls strength, weight, and all other parameters
  • Bend radius: Should be equal to or greater than thickness to avoid cracking
  • K-factor: Ratio of neutral axis depth to thickness, affects bend allowance
  • Bend allowance: Extra material needed to create a bend
  • Spring-back: Material tries to return to original shape after bending

Design Rules

  • Bend relief at partial bends (depth: 2T + R, width: T/2)
  • Corner relief where two bends meet
  • Holes placed at least 3T + r from edges and bends
  • Gussets to increase bend strength
  • Formed features for rigidity on large panels

Manufacturability was verified by flattening the part in SolidWorks to check for interference.

Plastic Design

I learned plastic design principles for non-load-bearing covers made using injection molding.

Design Approach

  • Surface features in SolidWorks to maintain constant wall thickness
  • Draft angles for easy mold release
  • Fillets at edges
  • Ribs for added strength without increasing thickness

Snap-Fit Assembly

Snap-fit features allow parts to be assembled without fasteners. This reduces cost, weight, and assembly time. The design uses interlocking tabs and detents that click into place.
